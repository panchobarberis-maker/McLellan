How It Works

The Rule Behind the Number

California requires final wages to be paid promptly. Immediately at termination, or within 72 hours if you resigned without notice.
If an employer willfully fails to pay everything owed at separation, Labor Code Section 203 allows a penalty of up to 30 days of your daily wages.
The clock counts calendar days, not working days, and it stops at 30 days no matter how long the delay runs.
The penalty runs regardless of how small the unpaid amount was, so a few hundred dollars in unpaid final wages can produce a penalty many times larger.
Willful means the employer intentionally failed to pay. A genuine, good faith dispute over what was owed can defeat the penalty.
Defective wage statements carry their own separate penalties under Labor Code Section 226, which this calculator does not estimate.

Waiting Time Penalty FAQs

It depends on how the employment ended. If your employer fired you or laid you off, all wages earned and unpaid are due immediately, at the time of termination. If you quit and gave at least 72 hours of notice, they are due on your last day. If you quit without notice, your employer has 72 hours. Those are the dates to enter above, and getting them right matters, because the penalty clock starts from the date payment was due rather than the date you asked for it.

Up to 30 days, and the days are calendar days rather than working days. If your final wages were 40 days late, the penalty still stops at 30 days of pay. If they were 6 days late, it runs 6 days. The cap is why the calculator stops climbing after a month, and it is also why a long delay and a very long delay are worth the same.

Willful does not require bad motive or malice. It generally means the employer intentionally failed to pay wages it knew were due. An employer that simply did not get around to it can still be liable. What can defeat the penalty is a genuine, good faith dispute about whether the wages were owed at all. An employer that argues in good faith and loses may still avoid the penalty, which is one reason the number this calculator produces is an estimate rather than an entitlement.

It can. The obligation is to pay all wages owed at separation, which includes accrued unused vacation, earned commissions that are calculable, and any unpaid overtime or premium pay. A partial payment that leaves something out can still trigger the penalty, and the penalty is measured by your daily wage rather than by the amount left unpaid. That is why a small shortfall can produce a penalty far larger than the shortfall itself.

Deadlines vary by the claim you bring and by whether you file with the Labor Commissioner or in court, and more than one deadline can apply to the same facts. Because the shortest applicable period controls, it is worth having the dates checked early rather than assuming you have time. If your separation was recent, that is the best moment to have someone look at it.
