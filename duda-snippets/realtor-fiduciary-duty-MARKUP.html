What Is a Realtor's Breach of Fiduciary Duty?

Understanding Breach of Fiduciary Duty by Real Estate Agents in California

Real estate agents and brokers in California owe their clients fiduciary duties, the highest standard of care recognized under the law.

This includes undivided loyalty, full and honest disclosure, and the duty to act in the client's best interest above their own. This is a distinct legal claim from breach of fiduciary duty by business partners or corporate officers, and it arises specifically from the agency relationship created when a buyer or seller retains an agent under a listing or representation agreement.

Claims against an agent commonly arise out of the same facts as purchase and sale disputes, and sometimes alongside CC&R violations that were never disclosed.

Common breaches of a real estate agent's fiduciary duty take several forms.

Common breaches include:

  • Failing to disclose a known material defect in the property.
  • Representing both the buyer and seller without proper written consent (undisclosed dual agency).
  • Steering a client toward a deal that benefits the agent's commission rather than the client.
  • Failing to present all offers received on a property.

California's licensing law, along with disclosure statutes specific to residential transactions, sets minimum standards agents must meet, but the fiduciary duty owed to their own client generally goes further than these baseline disclosure obligations.

An agent's breach of trust can cause substantial losses on what is often a person's largest financial decision.

Losses can range from overpaying for a property with undisclosed issues to losing a deal because an agent failed to communicate a competing offer. Claims against real estate professionals can also be reported to the California Department of Real Estate, but pursuing a civil claim is often necessary to recover the financial losses the breach actually caused.

Realtor Fiduciary Duty FAQs

A real estate agent generally owes their client undivided loyalty, confidentiality, full and honest disclosure of material facts, reasonable care and skill, and the duty to act in the client's best financial interest, even when it conflicts with the agent's own commission or convenience.

Dual agency occurs when one agent or brokerage represents both the buyer and seller in the same transaction. California law permits this only with informed written consent from both parties, because representing both sides creates an inherent conflict of interest that can compromise the agent's loyalty to either client.

Yes, if your agent knew about a material defect and failed to disclose it to you, this can support a breach of fiduciary duty claim, separate from any disclosure obligations owed by the seller. Agents are expected to disclose facts within their knowledge that could affect your decision to buy or the price you're willing to pay.

An agent generally has a duty to present all written offers to their seller client promptly, regardless of whether the agent believes the offer is worth considering. Failing to do so can constitute a breach of fiduciary duty, potentially costing the seller a better deal.

A fiduciary duty claim focuses on the trust-based relationship between an agent and their client, rather than just the terms of a listing agreement. This can allow for broader remedies and, in some cases, claims for the agent's self-dealing or conflicts of interest that a simple contract claim wouldn't reach.

Claims for breach of fiduciary duty are generally subject to a four-year statute of limitations, though claims rooted in fraud or concealment may be subject to a shorter period and a discovery rule that starts the clock when the breach was or reasonably should have been discovered.

These options are not mutually exclusive. A complaint to the California Department of Real Estate can result in professional discipline against the agent's license, but it does not compensate you for financial losses. A civil claim is generally necessary to recover damages caused by the agent's breach of duty.
