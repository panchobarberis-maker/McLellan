What Is a Non-Payment Dispute?

Understanding Non-Payment Claims in California

Non-payment disputes happen when a party fails to pay money it owes under a contract.

That can involve goods delivered, services rendered, a loan, or a commercial invoice. Non-payment is one of the most common forms of breach of contract, and California law generally entitles the unpaid party to recover the amount owed, plus any consequential losses caused by the delay or refusal to pay, and interest where the contract or statute provides for it.

These disputes take several common forms.

Common issues include:

  • Disputed invoices.
  • Unpaid vendor or contractor bills.
  • Defaulted loans or promissory notes.
  • Withheld commission or bonus payments.
  • Clients who refuse payment by claiming. sometimes in bad faith. that the underlying work was deficient.

Before pursuing a non-payment claim, it's important to confirm the contract terms, document the amount owed, and rule out valid offsets or defenses the paying party may raise, such as an alleged breach on your end.

Unresolved non-payment can strain cash flow and threaten a business's stability.

This is particularly true for small and mid-sized companies. California offers several paths to recovery. from a formal demand letter to a lawsuit for breach of contract or, in commercial settings, a common count claim for money had and received. and choosing the right strategy early often determines how quickly and completely you get paid.

California generally follows the 'American Rule,' meaning each side bears its own fees absent a contractual or statutory exception, so reviewing your agreement's fee clause is an important early step.

An open book account claim allows a business to recover a running balance of unpaid charges. common in ongoing vendor, supply, or service relationships. without needing to prove every individual transaction as a separate breach. It's often a faster and more efficient path to recovery than a traditional breach of contract claim.

Even with a strong claim, collectability matters. Before filing suit, it's worth assessing whether the debtor has assets, income, or insurance available to satisfy a judgment, since a judgment alone does not guarantee payment. enforcement may require additional post-judgment collection efforts.

Smaller matters can sometimes be pursued in small claims court without an attorney. But for larger commercial disputes, disputed defenses, or amounts beyond small claims limits, an attorney can pursue faster resolution, calculate recoverable interest and fees accurately, and respond effectively if the other side raises counterclaims.
