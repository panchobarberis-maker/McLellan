What Is a Will Contest?

Understanding Will Contests in California

A will contest is a formal legal challenge to the validity of a deceased person's will.

It's typically filed by an heir, beneficiary, or other interested party who believes the document does not reflect the decedent's true intentions. Rather than disputing how assets are divided, a contest attacks the document itself, arguing it should not be admitted to probate, or should be set aside, because of the circumstances under which it was signed.

California law recognizes several grounds for contesting a will.

Common grounds include:

  • Lack of testamentary capacity.
  • Undue influence by a caregiver or family member.
  • Fraud.
  • Duress.
  • Improper execution under the formalities required by the Probate Code.

Suspicious circumstances, such as a late-in-life change favoring one child over others, or a will drafted shortly after a new relationship began, often prompt closer scrutiny.

Timing matters enormously in will contests.

California imposes strict deadlines, and a contest filed after a will has already been admitted to probate faces a significantly shorter window than one filed before admission. Waiting too long can permanently forfeit your right to challenge a document you believe is invalid, which is why early legal guidance is critical.

Will Contest FAQs

California allows a will to be contested on grounds including lack of testamentary capacity, undue influence, fraud, duress, mistake, and improper execution. A successful contest typically requires more than suspicion, courts look for concrete evidence, such as medical records showing cognitive decline or communications revealing manipulation by a caregiver or family member.

If the will has not yet been admitted to probate, a contest can generally be filed at any point before that happens. Once the court admits the will, California law gives interested parties only 120 days to file a contest, making prompt action essential once you learn of a will you believe is invalid.

Undue influence occurs when someone uses their position of trust, often a caregiver, new spouse, or close family member, to manipulate a vulnerable person into changing their estate plan against their true wishes. Courts examine the decedent's dependency on the influencer, the fairness of the outcome, and whether the influencer was involved in procuring the will.

Only an 'interested party' may contest a will, generally someone who would inherit under a prior will or through intestate succession if the contested will were invalidated, or a beneficiary named in the disputed document. A person with no financial stake in the outcome typically cannot bring a contest.

If a court invalidates a will, the estate is typically distributed under a prior valid will, or if none exists, according to California's intestate succession laws. In some cases, only the specific provision found invalid, such as a suspicious late amendment, is struck while the rest of the document stands.

California enforces no-contest clauses only in limited circumstances, and courts construe them narrowly. Filing a contest with probable cause based on genuine evidence generally will not trigger forfeiture, but an attorney should review the specific clause and the strength of your claim before you file.
