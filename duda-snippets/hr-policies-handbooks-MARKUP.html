An employee handbook is one of the most effective, and most overlooked, tools for reducing litigation risk.

A well-drafted handbook clearly establishes at-will employment status, communicates required policies, sets consistent expectations across your workforce, and creates a documented record that supports your business if a dispute arises.

California imposes specific policy requirements that many off-the-shelf or outdated handbooks miss.

These requirements include:

  • Detailed meal and rest break policies.
  • Anti-harassment and complaint procedures that satisfy FEHA requirements.
  • Paid sick leave and protected leave policies (CFRA, PFL, and local ordinances).
  • Pay transparency and wage statement disclosures.
  • Reasonable accommodation procedures.

If your handbook and safety documents have not been reviewed since 2023, they are very likely missing requirements that are now mandatory.

Two are worth checking today. Under Labor Code section 6401.9, nearly every California employer must establish, implement, and maintain an effective written workplace violence prevention plan, kept available and easily accessible to employees at all times and specific to the hazards of each work area. The plan has to name who is responsible for it, set out how employees report incidents without fear of reprisal, address emergency response, and be reviewed at least annually and after any incident. It also requires a violent incident log for every incident, annual training for every employee, and record retention of up to five years. Separately, Labor Code section 6401.7 requires every employer to maintain a written injury and illness prevention program, and it now expressly lists a conforming workplace violence prevention plan as one of that program's required elements. So these are not two unrelated projects. If your injury and illness program predates the workplace violence requirement, it is incomplete by the terms of its own statute.

HR Policies & Handbook FAQs

California law does not universally require every employer to have a written handbook, but many specific policies, such as anti-harassment complaint procedures, paid sick leave notices, and certain leave policies, are effectively required in writing to satisfy your legal obligations and to give you a defensible record if a dispute arises. In practice, businesses without a current handbook are significantly more exposed in litigation because they lack documented, consistently communicated policies.

At minimum, California employers should have written policies covering anti-harassment and discrimination complaint procedures (required under FEHA regulations for most employers), paid sick leave, meal and rest break procedures, and reasonable accommodation processes. Depending on your size and location, additional requirements may apply, including local sick leave ordinances, pay transparency disclosures, and specific leave policies under CFRA or the Pregnancy Disability Leave law.

California's employment laws change enough that handbooks should be reviewed at least annually, and updated whenever a significant new law takes effect, which happens most years given the volume of employment legislation passed in California. A handbook that hasn't been reviewed in several years is very likely missing requirements that have since become mandatory.

Language that describes specific disciplinary steps, promises of continued employment, or detailed termination procedures can undermine at-will status and create an implied contract limiting your ability to terminate an employee without following those exact steps. A properly drafted handbook includes clear at-will disclaimers and avoids language that inadvertently promises a particular process.

Certain policies do need to distinguish between the two, meal and rest break policies, overtime eligibility, and timekeeping requirements generally apply only to non-exempt employees, and misapplying them (or failing to clearly define which employees they cover) is a common source of wage and hour exposure. A well-drafted handbook clearly identifies which sections apply to which classification.

An arbitration agreement can be referenced in a handbook, but California law generally requires it to be presented and signed as a standalone agreement, separate from routine handbook acknowledgment, to be enforceable. The agreement's terms also need to comply with specific California requirements to avoid being found unconscionable or unenforceable: this is an area where template language frequently falls short.
