What Is LLC & Partnership Dissolution?

Understanding Business Dissolution in California

Dissolution is the formal process of ending an LLC's or partnership's legal existence.

It involves winding up the business's affairs, paying off debts, and distributing remaining assets to the owners. Dissolution can be voluntary, by agreement of the owners, or involuntary, ordered by a court when owners are deadlocked or the business can no longer function.

Dissolution rarely happens quietly. It commonly arrives alongside shareholder disputes over control and breach of fiduciary duty claims between the owners.

A dissolution is often the final chapter of a broader business divorce.

It typically follows disputes over management, unequal contributions, or a breakdown in the working relationship between partners or members. How the process is handled can significantly affect what each owner walks away with.

California law sets out specific procedures for winding up a business, and deviating from them carries real risk.

The Corporations Code and Revised Uniform Limited Liability Company Act require:

  • Notice to creditors.
  • Asset liquidation.
  • Priority of distributions.

Deviating from these rules can expose owners to personal liability or delay the process significantly.

California law allows a member or partner to petition the court for involuntary dissolution when the business is deadlocked, when it's no longer reasonably practicable to carry on the business, or when those in control have acted illegally, fraudulently, or oppressively.

Outstanding debts must generally be paid, or provided for, before any assets are distributed to owners. Creditors are typically notified and given an opportunity to submit claims. Failing to properly notify creditors can expose owners to personal liability for the business's debts.

Valuation is often governed by the operating or partnership agreement; absent a formula, an independent business valuation is typically used, considering the company's assets, income, liabilities, and fair market value. Disagreements over valuation are one of the most common sources of dissolution disputes.
