The Basics

What Is Financial Elder Abuse?

Financial elder abuse is a form of exploitation that occurs when someone takes advantage of an elderly person's vulnerability to gain access to their finances or assets. Under California Welfare & Institutions Code § 15610.30, financial abuse can occur when a person or entity takes, obtains, appropriates, retains, or assists in taking an elder's or dependent adult's property for a wrongful use, with intent to defraud, or by undue influence. As California's elders continue to get older, sadly, so do the opportunities for those who would abuse them. Our attorneys have experience protecting elders from bad actors who engage in financial elder abuse. We also protect heirs who have seen their elders suffer at the hands of abusers. We litigate financial elder abuse cases, including through trial when a case needs to be tried. Every case turns on its own facts and past results do not predict future outcomes. What we can do is tell you honestly, in a free consultation, how we assess yours.

Financial abuse does not always involve outright theft. California law also recognizes abuse accomplished through undue influence, meaning excessive persuasion that overcomes a person's free will and produces an inequitable result. This commonly arises in disputed gifts, beneficiary changes, deeds, and trust amendments.

Common Forms

What Financial Elder Abuse Can Look Like

Financial elder abuse can take many forms, including unauthorized transfers or withdrawals, misuse of a power of attorney, taking title to an elder's home through deception or undue influence, pressuring an elder into gifts or loans, improper beneficiary or trust amendments, and misappropriation by a caregiver, relative, fiduciary, or financial professional, or by someone who assists another person in wrongfully obtaining the elder's assets.

What You Can Recover

What the Law Lets You Recover

California treats financial elder abuse more seriously than an ordinary claim for money, and the remedies reflect that. In addition to compensatory damages, a plaintiff who proves financial abuse is generally entitled to an award of reasonable attorney fees and costs, which means the cost of bringing the case is not necessarily borne by the family. And where property was taken in bad faith, California law provides for liability of twice the value of the property recovered. Double damages plus fees changes the arithmetic on cases that would otherwise be too small to pursue, and it is the reason a claim worth eighty thousand dollars is often worth bringing when a general commercial claim of the same size would not be.

Financial Elder Abuse FAQs

Attorneys can investigate the abuse, gather evidence, and identify the responsible parties. They can file lawsuits to reclaim misappropriated funds and seek damages for the victim. Under California law, elder abuse claims can lead to significant financial recovery and legal remedies. Legal professionals can also assist in securing protective orders and preventing further exploitation.

A California financial elder abuse action for damages is generally subject to a four-year limitations period running from when the plaintiff discovered, or through reasonable diligence should have discovered, the facts constituting the abuse. Other related claims may have different deadlines. Consulting with an attorney can ensure compliance with these timeframes and protect your rights.

No minimum dollar amount is required for a financial elder abuse claim. However, the conduct must satisfy California's statutory requirements, such as taking or retaining property for a wrongful use, with intent to defraud, or through undue influence. Even relatively small transactions may be actionable when those requirements are met, and the losses could grow in size if the problem is not addressed.

To prove financial exploitation of an elderly person, evidence is needed to demonstrate improper use of the elder's funds, property, or assets. This can include bank statements, financial records, and documents showing unauthorized transactions or transfers. Testimony from the elderly person, witnesses, and experts can also support the claim. Depending on the theory of liability, evidence of coercion, manipulation, undue influence, deception, or knowledge that the transaction would harm the elder, such as emails, texts, or recorded conversations, may also be important.

California's Elder Abuse and Dependent Adult Civil Protection Act generally protects California residents age 65 or older, as well as certain dependent adults ages 18 to 64 whose physical or mental limitations affect their ability to carry out normal activities or protect their rights.
