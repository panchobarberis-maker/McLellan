Contract Dispute FAQs

A contract is a legally binding agreement between two or more parties that creates mutual obligations enforceable by law. Contracts can be written or oral and must include an offer, acceptance, consideration, and mutual intent to be bound. They are essential in various transactions, including business deals, employment agreements, and sales of goods or services. In California, contracts must comply with state laws and regulations to be valid. Understanding the terms and conditions of a contract is crucial to ensure legal protection and enforceability.

An oral contract can be legally binding, but it is often harder to prove than a written contract. In California, certain agreements must be in writing, including many transfers of real property and agreements that, by their terms, cannot be performed within one year. Without written documentation, enforcing an oral contract relies on witness testimony and other evidence. Disputes over oral contracts can be more complex and challenging to resolve. It is advisable to document agreements in writing to ensure clarity and legal protection.

The statute of limitations refers to the time limit within which a lawsuit must be filed. In California, a breach-of-written-contract claim is generally subject to a four-year limitations period, while an oral-contract claim generally has a two-year period. The limitations period ordinarily runs from the breach, although accrual or tolling may differ in particular circumstances. If the lawsuit is not filed within this timeframe, the right to sue may be lost. Understanding these deadlines is crucial to protect your legal rights in contract disputes. The clock generally starts at breach, not at signing, and not when you discovered the breach. That distinction matters most in cases involving payment over time, because a contract that has been breached repeatedly may have more than one accrual date and some claims may still be live even if the earliest ones are not. If you are close to a deadline, that is a reason to call now rather than a reason not to.

California generally allows a non-breaching party to recover damages that place them in the position they would have been in had the contract been performed, often called expectation damages. Depending on the facts, this can include lost profits, costs incurred in reliance on the contract, and consequential damages that were foreseeable at the time of contracting. Punitive damages are generally not available for a straightforward breach, though a separate fraud claim may open that door. Attorney's fees may be recoverable if the contract or a specific statute authorizes them.

Many commercial contracts include such a clause, so it is worth checking the agreement before filing. Even without a contractual requirement, settlement discussions often happen alongside litigation, and a well-prepared demand letter can sometimes resolve a dispute without a lawsuit at all.

California generally enforces contractual attorney's fees provisions, and Civil Code section 1717 makes most one-sided fee clauses reciprocal, so the prevailing party can generally recover fees even if the clause only mentions one side. Without a fee clause or a statute that provides for fees, each side generally bears its own attorney's fees under the American Rule.

California generally enforces valid arbitration clauses, and a party with a mandatory arbitration provision in their contract is usually required to arbitrate rather than file suit in court, subject to certain exceptions and defenses. Arbitration can be faster and more private than litigation, but it also limits the right to appeal and can involve its own set of fees. Whether an arbitration clause applies to your specific dispute, and whether it is enforceable, is worth having an attorney review before you file anything.

Anticipatory breach occurs when one party clearly indicates, through words or conduct, that they will not perform their obligations under the contract before performance is actually due. California generally allows the other party to treat that repudiation as an immediate breach and pursue damages right away, rather than waiting for the performance date to pass. Whether particular conduct rises to the level of a clear repudiation is fact specific.
