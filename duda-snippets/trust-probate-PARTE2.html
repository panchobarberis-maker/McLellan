Deadlines

Deadlines in Trust and Probate Cases

Deadlines in trust and probate cases are shorter than people expect. If you received a formal notification from a trustee that a trust has become irrevocable, you generally have 120 days from the date that notification was served to bring a contest. That is a short window and it does not extend because you were gathering information or waiting on a family member to decide. Other deadlines in this area run on their own schedules, and some of them start from a date you may not have noticed at the time. If you have received anything on letterhead from a trustee, an executor, or a probate court, treat it as time sensitive and let us look at it.

Conservatorships

Conservatorship Services

When an adult can no longer manage their own finances or personal care, a court can appoint a conservator to do it for them. These cases are contested more often than people expect, usually because family members disagree about who should serve, or because a conservatorship is being sought over the objection of the person it would cover. We handle both sides, including petitions to remove or replace a conservator who is not acting in the conservatee's interest, and objections where a conservatorship is not the least restrictive option available. Trust & Probate FAQs

Probate is the legal process of administering a deceased person's estate, ensuring debts are paid and assets are distributed according to their will or state law. In California, probate involves validating the will, appointing an executor, and inventorying the estate's assets. This process can include paying outstanding debts and taxes before distributing the remaining assets to beneficiaries. Probate can be time-consuming and costly, depending on the estate's complexity. Understanding probate is crucial for effective estate planning and protecting your assets.

A trust is a legal arrangement where one party, known as the trustee, holds and manages assets on behalf of another party, called the beneficiary. Trusts can be used to manage assets during life and after death, provide continuity of management, and, when properly funded, help assets pass outside probate. Certain specialized trust structures may also serve tax-planning goals. In California, a trust may be revocable, meaning the settlor generally retains the power to revoke it, or irrevocable, meaning that power is limited or absent except as permitted by the trust or applicable law. Trusts are commonly used in estate planning to protect assets and ensure they are distributed according to the grantor's wishes. Understanding trusts can help in creating a comprehensive estate plan.

A trustee is an individual or entity appointed to manage and administer the assets held in a trust on behalf of the beneficiaries. The trustee has a fiduciary duty to act in the best interests of the beneficiaries, ensuring the trust's terms are followed. In California, a trustee's responsibilities include managing investments, distributing assets, and maintaining accurate records. Trustees must act with loyalty, care, and impartiality to fulfill their legal obligations. Understanding the role of a trustee is essential for effective trust administration and estate planning.

A trustee's fiduciary duties include acting in the best interests of the trust beneficiaries with loyalty, care, and impartiality. Trustees must manage trust assets prudently, avoid conflicts of interest, and ensure transparency by keeping accurate records and providing regular reports to beneficiaries. In California, these duties also require the trustee to follow the terms of the trust document and comply with relevant laws. Breach of fiduciary duty can lead to legal consequences and financial liability.
