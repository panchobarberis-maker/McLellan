Trials

Review all relevant documents and facts related to your case so you understand them thoroughly, and practice answering potential questions clearly and concisely, focusing on the truth. Dress professionally, arrive early, listen carefully to each question, and take your time to respond accurately without speculation. Consulting with your attorney beforehand can provide additional guidance.

Most cases do not go to trial; they typically settle before reaching that stage. Settlement negotiations and alternative dispute resolution methods, such as mediation or arbitration, often resolve disputes more efficiently, saving time and legal costs. Only a small percentage of cases proceed to trial.

You can file an appeal to have a higher court review the decision. In California, you must file a notice of appeal within a specific timeframe, typically 30 days for criminal cases and 60 days for civil cases. The appellate court will examine the trial record for legal errors that could have affected the verdict, so it's important to consult an appellate attorney to evaluate the merits of your case.

Costs can vary widely, ranging from tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of dollars, depending on the complexity of the case, the length of the trial, and attorneys' hourly rates. In California, costs may also include court fees, expert witness fees, deposition costs, travel, and document preparation.

Trusts & Estates

Probate is the legal process of administering a deceased person's estate, ensuring debts are paid and assets are distributed according to their will or state law. In California, probate involves validating the will, appointing an executor, and inventorying the estate's assets, and can be time-consuming and costly depending on the estate's complexity.

A trust is a legal arrangement where one party, the trustee, holds and manages assets on behalf of another party, the beneficiary. Trusts control how and when assets are distributed, often providing tax benefits and avoiding probate. In California, trusts can be revocable or irrevocable depending on whether they can be altered after creation.

A trustee is an individual or entity appointed to manage and administer the assets held in a trust on behalf of the beneficiaries. In California, a trustee's responsibilities include managing investments, distributing assets, and maintaining accurate records, and they must act with loyalty, care, and impartiality to fulfill their legal obligations.

A trustee's fiduciary duties include acting in the best interests of the trust beneficiaries with loyalty, care, and impartiality, managing trust assets prudently, avoiding conflicts of interest, and keeping accurate records with regular reports to beneficiaries. In California, these duties also require following the terms of the trust document and complying with relevant laws; breach can lead to legal consequences and financial liability.
