What Is a Purchase & Sale Dispute?

Understanding Purchase & Sale Disputes in California

Real estate purchase agreements govern some of the largest financial transactions most people or businesses will ever undertake.

Disputes can arise at nearly any stage — from a buyer backing out after removing contingencies, to a seller failing to disclose known defects, to disagreements over who is entitled to a deposit when a deal falls apart. Even well-drafted agreements can lead to litigation when the parties disagree about their obligations or the condition of the property.

California law requires sellers of residential property to disclose known material defects.

Failure to disclose can support claims for fraud, negligent misrepresentation, or breach of contract, in addition to remedies under the statutory disclosure requirements. When a party breaches a purchase agreement outright — such as refusing to close — the non-breaching party may seek:

  • Damages
  • Retention or return of the deposit
  • Specific performance, compelling the sale to proceed, because real property is considered legally unique

Purchase and sale disputes often involve significant sums and tight contractual deadlines for asserting claims or defenses.

Whether you are a buyer who discovered undisclosed problems after closing, or a seller facing a claim over a transaction you believed was handled properly, understanding your rights under the purchase agreement and applicable disclosure laws early can determine whether you recover fully, are compelled to complete a sale, or successfully defend against an unfounded claim.

Purchase & Sale Dispute FAQs

California requires sellers of most residential properties to disclose known material facts and defects that could affect the property's value or desirability, typically through a statutory disclosure form. Sellers must disclose known issues even if a buyer doesn't ask about them directly, and failing to do so can expose the seller to liability.

You may have claims for breach of contract, fraud, or negligent misrepresentation if the seller knew of a material defect and failed to disclose it. Document the defect, gather your disclosure paperwork and inspection reports, and consult an attorney promptly, as claims are subject to statutory deadlines.

It depends on the purchase agreement's terms and which party breached. If a buyer backs out without a valid contingency, the seller may be entitled to retain the deposit as liquidated damages. If the seller breaches or a valid contingency excuses the buyer's performance, the buyer is typically entitled to a full refund.

Claims for breach of a written purchase agreement generally must be filed within four years, while claims based on fraud or non-disclosure generally must be filed within three years of discovering the defect or misrepresentation, though the clock and available exceptions depend on the specific facts.

If a contingency — such as financing, inspection, or appraisal — is not satisfied and properly waived or removed, the party protected by that contingency generally has the right to cancel the agreement and receive a refund of their deposit, without breaching the contract.
