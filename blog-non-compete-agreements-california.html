When Non-Compete Agreements Are Enforceable in California

Claire Melehani, Esq. & Steven McLellan, Esq. · June 2026 · 8 min read

If you have been asked to sign a non-compete agreement in California, or if a former employer is threatening to enforce one against you, you need to know one thing before anything else: non-compete agreements are enforceable in California only in a narrow set of circumstances.

The California Rule: Non-Competes Are Generally Void

California Business and Professions Code section 16600 establishes one of the most protective employee mobility rules in the United States. As a general matter, every contract by which anyone is restrained from engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business of any kind is void.

Concrete example: A senior engineer leaves a Cupertino software company and accepts a role at a competitor. Her former employer sends a cease-and-desist letter citing a non-compete clause she signed at hire. Under section 16600, that clause is void — and the former employer's threat of litigation is, in most circumstances, an attempt to enforce an unenforceable restriction.

"California does not enforce non-compete agreements as a matter of public policy. The rule exists to protect employee mobility and California's competitive economy."

Recent Legislative Reinforcement: SB 699 and AB 1076

In 2023, the California Legislature passed two statutes that significantly strengthened the existing prohibition on non-compete agreements. Senate Bill 699 made it unlawful for an employer to enter into, maintain, or attempt to enforce a non-compete agreement that is void under California law, and extended that prohibition to agreements signed outside of California. Assembly Bill 1076 imposed an affirmative obligation on employers to notify current and former employees, by February 14, 2024, if those employees had signed non-compete agreements that were unenforceable under California law.

The Narrow Exceptions to Section 16600

California law recognizes a small number of statutory exceptions to the general prohibition on non-compete agreements. The sale of business exception permits a non-compete agreement in connection with the sale of a business, when the seller agrees not to compete with the buyer within a specified geographic area and for a specified period.

The dissolution of partnership exception similarly permits partners in a partnership to agree not to compete within a defined area upon dissolution of the partnership. A comparable provision applies to members of a limited liability company upon dissolution.

What Employers Can Protect: Trade Secrets and Confidential Information

Although California does not permit non-compete agreements, employers retain the right to protect legitimate business interests through other legal mechanisms. The California Uniform Trade Secrets Act prohibits the misappropriation of trade secrets, defined as information that derives independent economic value from not being generally known and that is subject to reasonable measures to maintain its secrecy.

Non-solicitation agreements directed at a former employer's employees are also void as a matter of California law following the California Supreme Court's decision in Ixchel Pharma, LLC v. Biogen, Inc., which confirmed that section 16600's prohibition extends beyond employee non-solicitation to any contract that restrains trade.

What to Do if Your Employer Is Threatening to Enforce a Non-Compete

Employees in San Jose, Cupertino, Los Gatos, or anywhere in Silicon Valley who receive a cease-and-desist letter or threat of litigation based on a non-compete agreement should not assume that the restriction is enforceable. In the vast majority of cases, the restriction is void under California law.
